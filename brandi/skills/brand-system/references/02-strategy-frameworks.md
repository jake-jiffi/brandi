# 02 · Strategy frameworks

The thinking layer. Everything here produces a decision, and every decision produces either a
line in the brand book or an entry in the open-questions register. Nothing here produces a
paragraph of strategy prose that nobody reads.

Read `01-evidence-protocol.md` first. Every claim generated by these frameworks carries a
provenance tag, and every pillar without proof becomes an open question rather than a
sentence.

---

## 1. The tiered question battery

Most brand processes fail at the intake. They open with twenty questions, the owner answers
four of them badly, and the whole system is built on a tired person guessing at their own
psychographics.

Three tiers. Only Tier 0 is asked. Tier 1 is Claude's job. Tier 2 is the register.

### TIER 0: the only thing asked up front

**Four questions. Under 90 seconds. Batched, presented together, answerable by tapping.**

Every question has two to four concrete options and an escape hatch. The escape hatch is not
a fallback: a free-text answer is always more useful than a tapped one, so never phrase it
apologetically and never treat it as a failure to answer.

---

**T0.1 · What are we doing here?**
- **A.** Starting from nothing. No logo, no colours, no name settled.
- **B.** Tidying up what exists. There is a logo and some colours, but everything looks
  slightly different depending on where you find us.
- **C.** Replacing what exists, deliberately. The current brand is wrong and we know it.
- **D.** Something else: `[  ]`

*Changes:* whether existing assets are constraints or candidates for retirement; whether §22
of the book carries a migration plan; whether logo work is in scope; how much of the book is
`EXTRACTED` versus `DECIDED`.

---

**T0.2 · Where does most of your work actually come from today?**
- **A.** Word of mouth and repeat customers.
- **B.** People searching and finding the website.
- **C.** Social media.
- **D.** A marketplace, a directory, a referral partner, or a platform.
- **E.** Honestly not sure, or it is a mix: `[  ]`

*Changes:* the application set in §17 (a word-of-mouth business needs a card, a sign and a
vehicle before it needs a social kit); which channel's evidence carries most weight in Tier 1;
which distinctive assets are worth investing in.

---

**T0.3 · When someone picks a competitor instead of you, it is usually because they are…**
- **A.** Cheaper.
- **B.** Bigger, better known, or feel safer.
- **C.** Closer or more convenient.
- **D.** They found them first. We were never in the running.
- **E.** Something else: `[  ]`

*Changes:* the competitive frame; whether the job is differentiation (A, B, C) or mental
availability (D); the points of parity you must cover before any point of difference matters.

---

**T0.4 · A year from now, which of these would you most want to be true?**
- **A.** People around here recognise us on sight.
- **B.** We can charge more than we do now without losing people.
- **C.** Everything we put out looks like it came from the same place.
- **D.** We can hand this to a developer or a printer and they get it right first time.
- **E.** Something else: `[  ]`

*Changes:* where craft budget goes. A is distinctive assets and applications. B is register,
photography and materiality. C is the system and the anti-patterns page. D is tokens,
specifications and the machine-readable layer.

---

**Rules for Tier 0:**
- Present all four at once. Do not conduct an interview.
- Never ask a fifth. If a fifth question feels essential, it is a Tier 1 question you have not
  worked out how to answer yet.
- Every option is a plain sentence a busy person recognises. No jargon, no "which best
  describes your brand personality", no sliders.
- A skipped question is `OPEN`, not a blocker. Proceed with the assumption tagged.
- Record every answer as `SUPPLIED` with the date.

### TIER 1: Claude answers these from evidence

These are never asked. Asking them is how brand processes die. Each has a source and a method,
and each produces a tagged finding the owner can correct in thirty seconds when they see it.

| Question | Evidence source | Method |
|---|---|---|
| What category do customers think you are in? | Review text, their Google Business Profile category, the search terms the site ranks for, how competitors self-describe | The category a customer names is often not the one the owner names. Note both, use theirs. |
| Who are the three genuinely dangerous competitors? | Local search, maps, category directories, review mentions | Three layers: direct (same problem, same way), indirect (same problem, different way), status quo (do nothing, do it themselves, live with it). The third is usually the real one and is usually forgotten. |
| What does the category all say? | Read six competitor homepages and the first line of each | Anything three or more of them say is a point of parity, not a differentiator. This list is the cliché register. |
| What language do customers use? | Their reviews, their enquiry emails, the questions in their DMs | Harvest verbatim phrases. Customer language and category language differ, and the gap is where the copy lives. |
| What do people actually praise? | Five-star review text, tagged by theme | A theme in three or more reviews is signal. A theme in one is anecdote. This is the cheapest proof-point source that exists. |
| What do people complain about? | One, two and three-star reviews, theirs and competitors' | Category-wide complaints are positioning territory. Their own complaints are a brand-truth constraint. |
| What is the current visual state? | Computed styles, logo files, photography, signage, vehicle, printed collateral | Everything measured, tagged `EXTRACTED`, dated. |
| Is the current name a constraint or an asset? | Search results for the name plus the category, existing recognition | Only escalates to Tier 2 if the answer is genuinely ambiguous. |
| What do they actually sell, in what mix? | Service list, price list, booking system if visible | Positioning built on the thing that makes 5% of revenue is a common and expensive error. |

**Show the Tier 1 findings back before building on them.** Not as questions, as statements
with a correction invitation: "Here is what I found. Tell me where I have it wrong." A person
who cannot answer "what is your positioning" can correct "your reviews say you are the person
who handles anxious dogs" in four seconds.

### TIER 2: becomes an open question

Anything Tier 1 cannot settle from evidence, and Tier 0 did not cover, does not get asked. It
gets registered per `01-evidence-protocol.md` §4, with a working assumption and a
recommendation.

Typical Tier 2 residents:
- Anything requiring internal data (repeat rate, average job value, margin by service, churn).
- Anything requiring a decision the owner has not made (are we opening a second location, are
  we hiring, do we want the wholesale channel).
- Anything requiring primary research (why lapsed customers left, unprompted brand recall,
  whether the colour is actually ownable in this category).
- Anything requiring professional advice (trade mark registrability, licensing claims,
  regulated-industry wording).

## 2. Positioning

### The statement

```
For [audience] who [situation or need],
[Brand] is the [competitive frame]
that [the benefit that matters most to them]
because [the reason it is credible].
Unlike [the main alternative], we [the specific difference].
```

The first four lines are the classic template popularised by Geoffrey Moore in *Crossing the
Chasm* (1991) and in general agency use since. It is a widely adopted industry convention, not
a research finding, and its value is entirely in the discipline of filling every slot with
something specific. The fifth line is the one most people skip and it is the one that does the
work.

Write three versions:
1. **Strategic.** Internal. Precise, unglamorous, filled slots.
2. **Human.** What the owner would say to a customer at the counter. This is the one that
   feeds the messaging hierarchy.
3. **Rejected.** The positioning you deliberately did not take, with one line on why. This
   goes in the decision log.

**Failure tests, run all four:**
- Could the three most dangerous competitors say this sentence with their name in it? If yes,
  it is not a position.
- Is the audience slot narrow enough to exclude someone? "People who want quality" excludes
  nobody.
- Is the reason-to-believe checkable? "Because we care" is not.
- Does the "unlike" name a real alternative rather than a strawman?

### The competitive frame

The frame is a strategic choice, not a fact. It determines what "better" means, so choosing it
casually is choosing the whole strategy casually.

Three layers, always:
- **Direct.** Same problem, same way.
- **Indirect.** Same problem, different way.
- **Status quo.** Doing nothing, doing it themselves, or living with it.

Three category definitions, always:
- **Stated.** What the business calls itself.
- **Actual.** What customers compare it against when deciding.
- **Opportunity.** An adjacent or narrower frame that would give more room.

A dog groomer's stated category is grooming salons. Their actual category, for most customers,
is the bath in the laundry on a Sunday. A frame of "salon versus salon" produces a fight over
price and parking. A frame of "salon versus your own bathroom" produces a different and better
brand.

### Points of parity and points of difference

From Kevin Lane Keller's brand positioning work (*Strategic Brand Management*), and it is the
single most useful distinction in this whole file.

- **Points of parity (POP).** What you must have to be considered at all. Failing a POP loses
  the sale. Winning a POP wins nothing.
- **Points of difference (POD).** The reason to pick you over the alternative. Must be
  desirable to the customer, deliverable by you, and not already owned by a competitor.

Almost every small business's stated differentiator is a POP. "Friendly service", "quality
work", "we're reliable", "we use good products", "we're passionate about what we do". These
are entry tickets.

**The test:** write the claim, swap in the two most dangerous competitors' names, and read it
aloud. If it survives the swap, it is a POP. Put it in the parity list (it still needs to be
covered, just not led with) and go find the actual difference.

Deliverable:

| | Claim | Type | Proof | Src |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Qualified groomers, insured | POP | Certificate held | `S` |
| 2 | Open Saturdays | POP | Two of six competitors also open | `E` |
| 3 | Handles anxious and reactive dogs, one dog at a time | POD | 6 of 22 reviews mention it unprompted | `P` |
| 4 | No cage drying, ever | POD | Stated policy, competitor scan found nobody else saying it | `S` + `E` |

### Territory scoring

When there are three to five candidate positions, score rather than vibe. Five criteria, 1 to
5 each.

| Criterion | 5 looks like | 1 looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **Distinctiveness** | No competitor in the frame is saying this | Three or more competitors say it on their homepage |
| **Credibility** | Provable today with evidence in hand | Aspirational, requires operational change first |
| **Relevance** | Customers raise it unprompted in reviews or enquiries | We think they should care |
| **Durability** | Still true in three years, survives growth | Tied to a current promotion or one staff member |
| **Defensibility** | Hard to copy: a real capability, a location, a body of work | Copyable in a week by anyone who reads the site |

Then two stress tests, both written down:
- **Pre-mortem.** "What would have to be true for this to work?" List it. If the list contains
  something that is not true and will not become true, the territory is fantasy.
- **Competitor response.** "If the biggest player copied this tomorrow, what happens?" If the
  answer is "we lose", the territory sits on a claim, not a capability.

### What this rejects

Every chosen position produces a rejection list. This is not optional and it is the section
people actually use.

```
This position says no to:
- Price-led messaging of any kind. No "affordable", no "competitive rates", no discount codes
  in the hero.
- Puppy and small-dog cuteness as the primary visual register.
- Volume language: "fast", "in and out", "while you wait".
- Any imagery of multiple dogs in a room together.
```

## 3. Distinctive brand assets, honestly

There is a large body of empirical marketing research here and a much larger body of folklore
that borrows its authority. Know which is which, because a client will quote the folklore at
you and you need to be able to answer without either repeating it or being rude about it.

### What the Ehrenberg-Bass work actually claims

Primary sources: Byron Sharp, *How Brands Grow* (Oxford University Press, 2010); Jenni Romaniuk
and Byron Sharp, *How Brands Grow Part 2* (2016); Jenni Romaniuk, *Building Distinctive Brand
Assets* (Oxford University Press, 2018). The Institute is at the University of South Australia.

The core claims, stated as they are actually made:

1. **Growth comes mainly from penetration.** Brands grow chiefly by being bought by more
   people, most of them light and infrequent buyers, rather than by increasing loyalty among
   existing buyers. Loyalty metrics tend to follow market share rather than drive it (the
   double jeopardy pattern).
2. **Mental availability.** The propensity of a brand to be noticed or thought of in buying
   situations. Built by linking the brand to the situations in which people buy (category
   entry points), across many buyers, consistently, over time.
3. **Physical availability.** Being easy to find and easy to buy. Frequently the larger
   constraint, and frequently the one nobody wants to talk about because it is unglamorous.
4. **Distinctive assets.** The non-brand-name sensory elements (colour, shape, character,
   typeface, sound, layout device, tagline) that trigger the brand in memory. Their job is
   recognition and attribution, not persuasion.
5. **Consistency compounds.** Assets acquire value through repeated, unchanged exposure.
   Refreshing the identity every two years destroys the asset you were building.
6. **The Distinctive Asset Grid** (Romaniuk). Two axes:
   - **Fame:** of category buyers shown the asset, the percentage who name the brand.
   - **Uniqueness:** of those who name *a* brand, the percentage who name only yours.

   Four positions, described by what to do rather than by label (secondary summaries use
   different names for the quadrants, so cite the behaviour, not a label you read on a blog):
   - **High fame, high uniqueness.** A real distinctive asset. Use it everywhere, change
     nothing about it.
   - **Low fame, high uniqueness.** Uncontested and unknown. Investable: it is yours if you
     put reach behind it.
   - **High fame, low uniqueness.** Well known and shared with the category. Using it hard
     builds the category, and often builds the market leader. Handle with care.
   - **Low fame, low uniqueness.** Not an asset. Either commit properly or drop it.

   Measuring fame and uniqueness properly requires a survey of category buyers. We are not
   running one. See the honest proxy below.

### What it does not claim

- It does **not** claim differentiation is worthless. It claims that *perceived* meaningful
  differentiation is weaker and rarer than marketers assume, and that distinctiveness
  (being recognisable) does more work than differentiation (being different) for most
  purchases. Those are different arguments and conflating them is the most common
  misapplication.
- It does **not** license "consistency is all that matters, content is irrelevant". Assets
  need reach to become famous. Consistency without reach is just repetition.
- It does **not** say positioning and strategy are pointless. It says they matter less than
  distribution and memory for the categories studied.
- The empirical base is overwhelmingly **large, repeat-purchase, mass-market categories** with
  panel data behind them. Applying it wholesale to a two-person suburban service business, a
  B2B enterprise sale with a nine-month cycle, or a once-in-a-decade purchase is an
  extrapolation, not a finding. It is a defensible extrapolation and it should be labelled as
  one.

### The honest proxy for a small brand

You cannot run a category-buyer survey for a dog groomer. You can do this, and you must label
it as what it is:

1. Shortlist two to four candidate assets (a colour, a shape, a character, a device, a phrase).
2. **Uniqueness proxy:** search the category plus the asset, and look at the first twenty
   local competitors. If three or more already use it, uniqueness is low. Record the count and
   the date. Tag `[EXTRACTED: competitor scan, N=20 | 2026-08-29]`.
3. **Fame proxy:** none exists without asking people. Assume fame is zero for a new asset and
   say so. `[ASSUMED: no measurement | OQ-NN]`.
4. Log an open question recommending the cheap real test: show the asset without the name to
   twenty customers over a fortnight and count who names the business.

Then apply the one rule that actually matters at this scale: **pick assets and stop changing
them.** The asset that gets used unchanged for five years beats the better asset that gets
refreshed twice.

### The folklore, named

State these plainly if a client raises them. Do not repeat them, and do not put them in a
document.

- **"Colour increases brand recognition by 80%."** Attributed everywhere to "a University of
  Loyola, Maryland study". Traced by Eddy Hagen (insights4print.ceo, 2019) to two papers by
  Dr Ellen Hoadley (1990, and a follow-up in 2000) on the effect of colour, font and bold
  highlighting on information processing in text and graphs, in an era of monochrome office
  printing. **The 80% figure does not appear in either paper, and neither paper is about brand
  recognition.** The claim is untraceable folklore. Do not use it. If pressed, the defensible
  and much duller statement is that colour is one of the more reliably recognised distinctive
  assets, per Romaniuk's work.
- **"It takes 5 to 7 impressions to recognise a logo."** No locatable primary source. Do not
  use.
- **"People judge a product in 90 seconds and 62 to 90% of that judgement is colour alone."**
  No locatable primary source. Every citation leads to another secondary citation. Do not use.
- **"Consistent brand presentation increases revenue by 23%."** Circulates widely with no
  locatable primary source or published methodology. Do not use.
- **The 60-30-10 colour rule.** Interior-design convention, not research. Useful as a
  proportion heuristic. Present it as a house convention, not a finding.

**House rule: no statistic goes into a brand document without a primary source, a date and a
URL. If you cannot produce all three, the sentence is written without the number, or it is not
written.**

## 4. Brand archetypes

### The twelve

| Archetype | Core drive | Voice signature | Failure when overplayed | Over-used in |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Innocent** | Safety, simplicity, goodness | Warm, plain, optimistic, short | Naive, saccharine, evasive about hard things | Wellness, baby, organic |
| **Sage** | Truth, understanding | Measured, precise, explanatory | Lecturing, cold, condescending | B2B SaaS, consulting, education |
| **Explorer** | Freedom, discovery | Restless, first-person, sensory | Self-absorbed, aimless | Outdoor, travel, DTC apparel |
| **Outlaw** | Disruption, liberation | Blunt, adversarial, short sentences | Edgy for its own sake, adolescent | Crypto, energy drinks, challenger fintech |
| **Magician** | Transformation | Visionary, metaphor-heavy, promissory | Vague, over-promising, mystical | AI, beauty, "platforms" |
| **Hero** | Mastery, proving worth | Direct, muscular, imperative | Aggressive, tone-deaf, bragging | Sports, security, recruitment |
| **Lover** | Intimacy, beauty | Sensory, unhurried, second-person | Cloying, exclusionary | Luxury, hospitality, fragrance |
| **Jester** | Enjoyment, present moment | Quick, irreverent, playful with form | Unserious when it matters, dated | Snacks, gaming, budget travel |
| **Everyman** | Belonging, connection | Conversational, self-deprecating, plain | Bland, forgettable, apologetic | Retail, insurance, community brands |
| **Caregiver** | Service, protection | Patient, reassuring, specific | Patronising, martyred, anxious | Health, childcare, aged care, pets |
| **Ruler** | Control, order | Formal, declarative, authoritative | Pompous, rigid, remote | Finance, law, premium automotive |
| **Creator** | Self-expression, craft | Detailed, process-loving, opinionated | Precious, inward-facing | Studios, tools, makers |

### The real provenance

Carl Jung wrote about archetypes as recurring patterns in the collective unconscious. He was
doing analytical psychology, not marketing, and he never produced a list of twelve for brands.
The twelve-archetype brand model comes from Margaret Mark and Carol S. Pearson,
*The Hero and the Outlaw* (McGraw-Hill, 2001), building on Pearson's earlier
*Awakening the Heroes Within* (1991).

**State it plainly, including to clients: archetypes are a useful generative prompt, not
validated science.** There is no body of evidence establishing that a brand assigned an
archetype outperforms one that is not, and the number twelve is a convention rather than a
finding. What archetypes are genuinely good at is forcing a single character choice and making
copy briefs concrete. "Write it as a Caregiver, not a Hero" is a usable instruction.
"Write it warmly but confidently" is not.

**How to use them:**
1. Pick one primary. Optionally one secondary. Never three.
2. Name the failure mode you are guarding against. If you cannot, you have picked a mood, not
   an archetype.
3. Use it to generate the voice attributes, the imagery direction and the copy samples.
4. **Then throw the label away.** The archetype is scaffolding. It goes in the decision log as
   the reason for a set of choices. It does not go on a page in the brand book as a claim
   about the brand's nature, and it certainly never appears in customer-facing copy.

## 5. Messaging hierarchy

Four levels. Each level is the level above it with more room. If a lower level says something
the top level contradicts, the strategy is broken, not the copy.

**Level 0 · The promise.** One line, 8 to 14 words, in the customer's language, not the
category's. Not a tagline. This is the strategic anchor that everything else derives from.

```
Format: [Brand] helps [audience] [outcome] by [how].
```

**Level 1 · Three pillars.** The three things the brand says, over and over, forever. Each is
a bold claim of five to eight words. Three is the number: two is thin, four gets remembered as
none.

**Level 2 · Proof per pillar.** Minimum two admissible proof points each.

**Level 3 · The proof bank.** Six to ten specific, checkable facts available to any piece of
copy. This is what stops writers inventing.

### The proof rule

**Every pillar needs evidence or it becomes an open question.** No exceptions, no "we'll get
the proof later".

Admissible proof:
- A demonstrable product or service fact (something a customer can observe)
- A verifiable credential, licence, qualification or insurance
- A countable operational fact the business can produce from its own records
- A named customer with recorded permission
- A third-party rating, with platform, value and capture date
- A guarantee, warranty or policy the business actually offers and honours
- A verbatim customer quote from a public review, quoted accurately with its source

Not admissible:
- Adjectives. "High quality", "professional", "caring".
- Intentions. "We're committed to…", "We believe in…"
- Awards you cannot name and date
- "Years of experience" without a start year
- Anything that would need to be tagged `[ASSUMED]`

**A pillar that survives this filter with zero proof is demoted.** It becomes a hypothesis in
the register, with a recommendation naming the cheapest way to get evidence, and it does not
appear in customer-facing copy until it has some. Say this to the client directly: it is
usually the most valuable single sentence in the engagement.

### Channel adaptation

| Channel | Which pillar leads | Length | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Website hero | Level 0, verbatim | 8 to 14 words | The promise, not a tagline |
| Google Business Profile | Level 0 plus pillar 1 | 750 chars | Where local search actually reads |
| Instagram bio | Pillar 1, compressed | 150 chars | Plus the one thing to do next |
| Email subject | One specific fact | 6 to 9 words | Never the promise, never a tease |
| Sign or vehicle | Category plus one distinguishing word | 3 to 6 words | Legible at 20 metres and 60km/h |
| Sales conversation | The human positioning variant | Spoken | The owner has to be able to say it |
| Paid ads | Pillar plus one proof point | Platform limit | Proof point is what makes it work |

### Things not to say

Every messaging hierarchy ships with the banned list. Populate it from the Tier 1 category
scan (anything three or more competitors say), the cliché register, and any claim that failed
the proof rule.

## 6. Naming

**Gate: run this only when the business genuinely has no name, or has explicitly decided to
change one.**

If a name exists and is in use, it has accrued recognition you cannot see and the client
cannot easily value. Changing it is an equity decision with real cost (signage, vehicles,
search history, word of mouth, the regulars who tell their friends). Renaming a going concern
because the name is a bit plain is one of the more expensive mistakes in this trade. If the
question comes up, ask what problem the name is causing, and only proceed if there is one.

### Generation

Eight approaches. Generate across at least four of them, never one.

| Approach | What it is | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Descriptive | Says what it is | General Electric, American Airlines |
| Evocative | Suggests a quality | Patagonia, Stripe, Oasis |
| Founder | A person's name | Ford, Disney |
| Acronym | Initials of a longer phrase | IBM, BMW |
| Coined | An invented word | Kodak, Asana |
| Metaphor | A borrowed concept | Apple, Amazon, Twitch |
| Compound | Two words joined | PayPal, Facebook |
| Suggestive | Hints at the function | Tide, Slack |

Volume: 30 to 50 candidates across four or more approaches, filtered to 8 to 12, then three
finalists with a full case each. Working lists are internal. The client sees the finalists,
not the kill file, though the kill file is kept.

**The AI slop patterns.** If a name matches one of these, it is not a candidate.

| Pattern | Examples |
|---|---|
| Suffix addiction | Groomly, Petify, Washable, Barkora |
| Meaningless portmanteau | Petrix, Nexagroom, Vaultora |
| Thesaurus extraction | Apex, Pinnacle, Summit, Zenith |
| Category plus modifier | CloudGroom, PetSync, DogWatch |
| Vacant corporate language | Anything Solutions, Labs, Group, HQ |
| Fake Latin or Greek | Canivex, Groomiq, Luminex |
| Aspirational adjectives | BrightPaw, TrueGroom, ClearCoat |

Filler first words: Smart, Auto, Cloud, AI, Data, Next, Digital, True, Pure, Open, Fast, Easy,
Pro, Ultra, Hyper, Super, Meta, Omni, Core.
Generic second words: Hub, Lab, Base, Flow, Sync, Link, Stack, Forge, Shift, Verse, Craft,
Wise, Path, Nest, Box, Co.

The diagnostic: **if the name works equally well for three different products in three
different categories, it is slop.**

### Screening checklist

Run in this order. Stop at the first hard kill.

**1. Prior art, first, always.** This is the most-skipped and most fatal step. A free domain
or a free handle says nothing about whether a prominent business already uses the word.

- Web search: `"[name]" [category]`, `"[name]" [city]`, `"[name]" business`
- Local: Google Maps for the name in the trading area and the surrounding suburbs
- Registers: ASIC business name search, ABN Lookup, and the company register
- Trade marks: **IP Australia's Australian Trade Mark Search** for AU. Add USPTO TESS and
  EUIPO eSearch if the business will ever trade or ship there.

**Kill rules:**
- Same category, same trading area: **dead**. No exceptions.
- Same category, different city, with a website: **dead** for anything that will rank in
  search.
- Registered trade mark in a relevant class: **dead**, and refer to an attorney before
  spending anything.
- Large brand in an unrelated industry: usable but penalised. You will fight for search
  results permanently.

**2. Trade mark class search.** Identify the Nice classification classes the business actually
trades in (for a grooming salon, class 44 covers animal grooming services; class 31 covers pet
food; a merchandise line adds class 25). Search the mark in those classes and the neighbouring
ones. **This is a knockout search only.** See the disclaimer below.

**3. Domain.** The `.com.au` or `.com` for the exact name. `.com.au` requires an Australian
presence, typically an ABN or ACN. If the presence claim rests on a trade mark alone, the
domain must exactly match the mark. Check current auDA licensing rules before relying on this.
If the exact match is gone, test `get[name].com.au` and `[name][category].com.au` rather than
mangling the spelling. A misspelling forced by domain availability is a permanent tax on
word of mouth.

**4. Social handles.** Instagram and Facebook at minimum. Consistency across platforms matters
more than getting the exact string on any one of them.

**5. Existing-use web check.** Search the name in quotes, alone. Read the first two pages. You
are looking for anything embarrassing, anything confusable, and anything that will outrank the
business forever.

**6. Say it and spell it.** Read it aloud to five people who have not seen it written. If
three pronounce it differently, fail. Ask five people to spell it from hearing. If two get it
wrong, fail. Then run it through the sentence tests, because names live in sentences:

```
"Have you tried ___?"
"We just booked ___."
"___ is a [category] in [suburb]."
"You should try this place called ___."     ← the most important one
```

**7. Negative associations.** Translations and slang in any language spoken in the trading
area. Acronym check. The run-together spelling check (the classic failure is a compound that
reads as something else when the space disappears).

### Not legal advice

**Stating this plainly is part of the deliverable, not a disclaimer to bury.**

An agent's trade mark search is a knockout search: it finds obvious conflicts, and it cannot
tell you whether a mark is registrable, which classes you actually need, whether a similar
mark is confusingly similar in law, or whether you are exposed to an infringement claim.
Registrability, class selection, clearance opinions and oppositions are the work of a
registered trade marks attorney. Registering a business name with ASIC gives you the right to
trade under it and **confers no exclusive rights whatsoever**; only a registered trade mark
does that, and the two are routinely confused.

Recommend counsel before the client spends money on signage, vehicle wraps, packaging or
anything else that is expensive to redo.

### Tier 2, registered

- **OQ-01** Repeat rate. Assumed high from review language. High priority: pillar 2 leans on it.
- **OQ-02** Revenue mix across grooming, wash-only, and puppy first-groom. Medium: positioning
  should lead with the service that pays, and we do not know which it is.
- **OQ-03** Is the green ownable locally? Two of the twenty local businesses scanned use a green.
  Medium, with a recommendation to test.

### Frame and parity

- **Stated category:** professional pet styling.
- **Actual category:** somewhere to take the dog that will not make it worse.
- **Opportunity category:** grooming for dogs that find grooming hard.

That third frame is narrower, excludes cheerful golden retrievers who do not care, and is
uncontested locally. It was chosen deliberately, with the exclusion written down.

| 1 | Qualified and insured | POP | Certificate and policy held | `S` |

| 3 | One dog in the salon at a time | POD | Operating policy, verified against the booking calendar | `S` |

### Positioning

**Strategic:**

> For Brunswick dog owners whose dog finds grooming stressful, The Wash House is the salon
> that takes one dog at a time, because there is never another dog in the room and never a
> cage dryer. Unlike the walk-in salons, we book a two-hour slot for a job that takes ninety
> minutes.

**Human:**

> One dog at a time. No cages, no drying room, no other dogs barking. Your dog is the only one
> here.

**Rejected:** "Brunswick's friendliest groomers." Rejected because friendliness is a point of
parity, all four competitors claim it, and it gives no reason to drive past two cheaper salons.
Logged as `DL-002`.

**Scoring the chosen territory:** Distinctiveness 5 (nobody local says it) · Credibility 5
(true today, verifiable from the calendar) · Relevance 4 (6 of 22 reviews raise it unprompted,
though only among people who already come) · Durability 3 (it caps capacity, so it constrains
growth, which is a real strategic cost and was discussed) · Defensibility 4 (a competitor
copying it must halve their throughput).

**Pre-mortem:** this only works if the price supports half the throughput. That was raised with
Nadia and became a live business conversation, which is the point of a pre-mortem.

**Rejection list:**

```
- No price or discount messaging anywhere in the identity.
- No "fur baby", "pamper", "spa day", or any of the category's affection vocabulary.
- No photography with more than one dog in frame.
- No cute-puppy stock. Real clients' dogs, real salon, or nothing.
- No exclamation marks in body copy.
```

### Distinctive assets

| Candidate | Uniqueness proxy | Fame | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deep green `#2E5A50` | 2 of 20. Under the three-competitor threshold, so not disqualified, but green is the category's safe colour and the margin is thin. `[E: scan N=20 \| 2026-08-29]` | Unmeasured `[A \| OQ-03]` | Keep, but it is not the lead asset |
| The hand-lettered wordmark | Unique. Nobody else is hand-lettered. | Unmeasured, but it is on the sign and the van already | **Lead asset.** Redraw as vector, change nothing else |

The silhouette row is the useful one. It is the thing every groomer's designer reaches for
first, and it is the thing that guarantees she looks like everyone else.

### Archetype

**Caregiver primary, Everyman secondary.** Guarding against the Caregiver failure mode:
patronising, anxious, martyred. The tell would be copy that fusses at the reader or performs
concern. What it generated: patient sentence rhythm, specific reassurance instead of general
warmth ("you will get a photo when the dryer goes off", not "we'll take great care of them"),
and imagery of calm rather than imagery of affection.

The label went into `DL-004` with that rationale. It appears nowhere in the brand book as a
claim and nowhere in customer copy at all.

### Messaging hierarchy

**Level 0:** The Wash House helps Brunswick dog owners get their dog groomed without the day
becoming a problem.

| Pillar | Claim | Proof | Src |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | One dog in the salon at a time | Booking calendar shows single-slot bookings only. Policy stated on the door. | `S` `E` |
| 2 | No cage drying, ever | Policy. No cage dryer on the premises (photographed). | `S` `E` |
| 3 | ~~We remember your dog~~ | 5 of 22 reviews mention it by name, which is evidence of the impression, not of the practice. Nadia keeps notes but there is no system. | **demoted** |

Pillar 3 was demoted to `OQ-04` with a recommendation: if the notes become a per-client record
in the booking system, it becomes provable in a fortnight and can be promoted. Until then it is
not said in customer-facing copy. Nadia's reaction to that was to set the system up, which is
the best outcome this process produces.

**Proof bank:** two-hour slots for ninety-minute jobs · no cage dryer on site · certified and
insured (cert number held) · 4.9 across 22 Google reviews, captured 2026-08-29 · on Sydney
Road since [YEAR, ask Nadia] · handles reactive dogs, six unprompted review mentions.

**Things not to say:** fur baby · pamper · spa day · pooch · doggo · "we treat them like our
own" · anything with an exclamation mark · any price framing · "professional pet styling".
